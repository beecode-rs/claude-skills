## Good questions vs bad questions

A question passes the Mom Test when even your mom couldn't lie to you about the answer - because it asks about her life and her concrete past, not about your idea and her opinions.

| Bad question (invites lies) | Why it fails | Mom-test rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| "Do you think it's a good idea?" | Opinion about your idea; everyone says yes to be nice | "What are you currently doing to deal with ___? Talk me through the last time." |
| "Would you buy a product that did X?" | Hypothetical future; people are optimistic liars about their future selves | "What have you already tried to solve this? What did it cost you?" |
| "How much would you pay for X?" | Imaginary number, zero commitment behind it | "How much does this problem cost you today?" / "What do you pay for [current tool]?" |
| "Would you use a feature that...?" | Future + your idea; answer will be an enthusiastic worthless yes | "Why do you want that? What would it let you do that you can't do now?" |
| "Do you ever have problems with ___?" | Leading; hands them the problem to agree with | "What's the hardest part of [doing the broader activity]?" |
| "Do you often ___?" | Invites "always/usually" fluff | "When was the last time that happened? Walk me through it." |

Workhorse questions that almost always pass:

- "Talk me through the last time ___ happened."
- "What's the hardest part about ___?" then "Why is that hard?" (the *why* is where the product insight lives - people pay for benefits, not features)
- "What are you doing about it now?" - if the answer is "nothing," the problem probably doesn't matter, no matter how enthusiastically they agree it exists
- "What have you already tried?" - Googled it? Bought something? Built a spreadsheet? Effort spent is the best proxy for pain
- "How much does the problem cost you?" - money, hours, stress
- "Where does the money/budget for this come from? Who else is involved in deciding?" (B2B - finds the real buyer)
- "What else should I have asked?" / "Who else should I talk to?" - great closers; the second one also grows the interview pipeline

Digging follow-ups (use relentlessly): "Why?" "How so?" "What happened next?" "Can you show me?" "When was the last time?"

## The three types of bad data

### 1. Compliments
"That's really cool!" "I love it!" - Worthless. Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: they feel like validation and mean nothing. If a conversation produced mostly compliments, the interviewer was pitching, not learning.

**Deflect:** brush it off, return to their life. "Thanks - but back up a second: you said you handle this with a spreadsheet today. Walk me through that?"

### 2. Fluff
Three flavors:
- **Generic claims:** "I usually...", "I always...", "I never...", "I would totally..."
- **Future promises:** "I would definitely buy that", "We're planning to fix this next quarter"
- **Hypothetical maybes:** "I might", "I could see myself..."

Fluff isn't lying, exactly - it's people describing an idealized self. It becomes dangerous when treated as fact.

**Deflect:** anchor to a specific past instance. "You said this happens all the time - when was the last time? What did you do?" A fluffy positive that survives anchoring becomes a fact; one that doesn't was noise.

### 3. Ideas (feature requests)
"You should add X!" "It would be great if it integrated with Y!" - Ideas are data about *their* problem, not instructions for your roadmap. Founders drown in well-meant feature requests.

**Deflect by digging into the motivation:** "Why do you want that?" "What would that let you do?" "How are you coping without it?" "Should we drop everything to build it, or can it wait?" The underlying job-to-be-done is the data; the feature idea itself is discardable packaging.

### Bonus trap: the interviewer seeking approval
If the user reports fishing ("...don't you think that'd be great?") or pitching mid-interview ("but it also does X!"), name it in the debrief. Wanting approval is human; it's also how you buy compliments with interview time.

## Commitment and advancement

Once the conversation stops being casual discovery and a product is actually on the table, opinions stop counting entirely. A meeting has only two good outcomes:

- **Commitment** - they give up something of value:
  - **Time:** a scheduled follow-up with a clear agenda, hours spent trying a prototype, a workshop
  - **Reputation:** intros to their boss, their team, other customers; a public testimonial
  - **Money:** a pre-order, a deposit, a signed letter of intent, a paid pilot
- **Advancement** - the deal moves to a concrete next step toward a sale/adoption.

Everything else is a polite rejection. **"Sounds great, keep me posted!"** = no. **"Let me know when it launches"** = no. **"I'd definitely use that"** = no. The absence of a concrete next step *is* the answer.

Rule of thumb: the more someone gives up, the more seriously to take what they say. And always *ask* for the commitment - meetings fail by default when the interviewer, fearing the no, never asks. A clear "no" is the second-best possible outcome; "maybe" is the worst.

## Customer slicing

When a segment is too broad, you get conflicting signals from people who have nothing in common, and you can't find them to begin with. Slice repeatedly:

- Within this group, which sub-group **wants this most**?
- Which sub-group would **pay first** / has budget?
- What are they **already doing** to solve it? (People already paying/hacking a solution are the beachhead.)
- **Where do they gather?** Specific communities, forums, Slack/Discord groups, meetups, conferences, subreddits.

Stop slicing when the segment is: specific enough that two members would give similar answers, findable in a known watering hole, and big enough to matter later. "Developers" → "freelance web developers" → "freelance web developers who juggle 3+ client projects and invoice manually" - now you know exactly who to DM and where.

If interviews keep giving contradictory answers, that's usually a slicing failure, not a research failure: re-slice and re-target rather than averaging the answers.

## Keeping it casual

- Early problem-discovery conversations should not feel like interviews. No "can I interview you for my startup" - that summons the politeness field. Instead: seek advice. "You know this space better than me - how do you deal with ___?"
- 5 casual minutes at a meetup can beat a 30-minute scheduled call - there's no performance, so there's no lying.
- Don't mention the idea at all in early conversations. The idea's absence is what keeps the data clean.
- Formal meetings become appropriate later, when there's something concrete on the table (a prototype, a pilot, a price) - and then the goal shifts to commitment, not conversation.

## Note-taking signal glossary

Suggest these symbols for live notes (from the book, adapt as the user likes):

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ⚡ | Pain or problem |
| 🎯 | Goal or job-to-be-done |
| 🔨 | Workaround / current solution |
| 💰 | Money: budget, spend, cost of problem |
| 😠 😍 | Strong emotion (anger, excitement, embarrassment) - always dig here |
| 💡 | Feature request / idea (dig for the why) |
| ☁️ | Fluff - vague/future/hypothetical claim |
| ⭐ | Follow-up: promised intro, next step, commitment |
| "..." | Exact quote worth keeping verbatim |

Exact quotes and specific numbers are the most valuable notes: "spends ~4h/month reconciling invoices, called it 'the worst day of my month'" is evidence; "has invoicing problems" is barely anything.
